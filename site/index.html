Why Nine Arches

In the hills near Ella, a railway needed to cross a valley.

The line curves high above the tea, where a bridge would have to be strong, beautiful and right for the people riding over it.

Completed in 1919, it was built by local workers under British engineers.

Concrete, stone and brick, raised pier by pier.

Local legend says it was built without steel.

It credits a village builder, P. K. Appuhami.

Told in Ella as a legend; the full history is debated.

About 91 metres long and 24 metres high.

It's still carrying trains more than a century later.

Careful design and skilled hands, for the people who use it. That's what we're building for.

Nine Arches tests a building with the people who will use it, before it's built, so it can work as well on opening day as that bridge still does today.

The problem

Most buildings meet their people on opening day.

By then, the corridor that jams at the bell, the toilet nobody can reach without steps, the exit everyone walks past… they're built in concrete. Fixing them costs months, money, or both.

How it works

Let the people in early.

Four steps, one plan, and a day of people telling you what works.

  1. 1

    Draw it, or trace it.

    Sketch walls on a blank page, trace a photo or PDF of an existing plan, or import a CAD drawing. Or just describe it to Ella.

  2. 2

    Fill it with people.

    Say who uses the building and when: staff, students, patients, visitors, residents. Each one is a little different: walking speed, how well they know the place, how much crowding they'll put up with.

  3. 3

    Watch a day happen.

    Press play. People arrive, move, wait, meet and leave. Heatmaps show where they crowd, how long they wait, and where it's loud, dim or glary.

  4. 4

    Fix it, and try again.

    Move a wall, add a door, or ask Ella. Run the day again and compare before and after, side by side.

What you can see

Every way a building can work, or not.

Watch the day as it happens, then look at it through different lenses.

People moving

Every person walks their own way, at their own pace, to the places their day takes them.

Crowding

See where it gets tight, and when.

Waiting

Queues at lifts, counters, toilets and doors.

Step-free access

Who can get where, without a step.

Light and glare

Where it's dim, and where the sun is in someone's eyes.

Noise

Where it's loud enough to make focus hard.

Alarm

When the alarm sounds.

Watch everyone leave, see which exits they choose, and how long the last person takes.

On site

On the real map, under the real sun.

Pin your building on the map. Nine Arches brings in the streets and neighbouring buildings around it, so you can move and turn it into place, see what it replaces, and watch the shadows cross it through the day and the year.

  • Neighbours, live. Move your building and the ones it would replace step aside, in real time.
  • Sun and shade. The sun's real path for that place and date, with shadows from the buildings around you.
  • Real weather. Typical cloud, temperature and rain for the site, from historical records.
